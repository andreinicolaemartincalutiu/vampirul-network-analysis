Undead Networks: reading the undead at a distance, together

What the project does, what it aims for, how it works, and how researchers working on vampires, revenants and the Gothic can join it.

Abstract

Undead Networks is a digital humanities project, based in Sibiu, Romania, that studies vampire, strigoi and revenant fiction through computational models validated by researchers. Each work in the archive becomes a dataset of who speaks, to whom, in whose silent presence, with what words, and, increasingly, of what characters do to one another. From these data we build interactive character networks, vocabulary profiles, narrative-event layers and translation comparisons, published side by side so that works from different languages and traditions can be compared. The pipeline is open source. This whitepaper sets out the project's aims and methods and invites researchers to join it.

1. Context

Why the undead, and why at a distance

The undead are among the most mobile figures of modern literature. The strigoi of Romanian folklore, the aristocratic vampire of the Gothic, the revenants of the pulp magazines and the political vampires of satire move between languages, media and centuries, and each tradition reshapes them. Comparing these traditions is a central task for scholars of horror, yet comparison usually rests on a handful of canonical texts read closely.

A vampire story is also, almost always, a story about relationships: a household fed upon, a victim chosen, a community infected, a monster known mostly through what others say of it. These are social structures, and social structures can be modelled. Distant reading does not replace close reading; it gives it a map. A network shows where the monster sits in its world; the text tells us what that position means.

What has been missing is a shared, consistent way to produce such maps across a growing corpus, with the interpretive decisions made by people who know the texts, and with every decision documented.

2. What we do

An archive of comparable analyses

We build and publish a growing archive of undead fiction, each work processed by the same pipeline and presented in the same interactive visualizer:

  • Character networks: weighted graphs of direct dialogue and co-presence, with centrality measures, communities, gender and, where relevant, social rank.
  • Vocabulary profiles: lexical diversity, part-of-speech patterns, and the words each character uses with particular partners.
  • Narrative events: acts extracted from the narration, with agent, patient and sense. The first is predation (who bites, drains, feeds on or bleeds whom, literally or figuratively), introduced with Thomas M. Sipos's Vampire Nation.
  • Translation comparisons: two translations of the same work aligned dialogue by dialogue and compared in tone, starting with Karl von Wachsmann's Der Fremde.
  • Literary geography (in development): settings, character mobility and networks of places.

The archive currently spans Romanian novels built around the strigoi and the vampire, international fiction set in Romania, German Gothic, and American pulp fiction. Browse the archive.

3. What we aim for

Aims

  1. A comparative, transnational corpus. Enough works, processed consistently, to compare how Romanian, German, British, American and other traditions organise the world around the undead.
  2. Answerable research questions. Where does the monster sit in its social world? Who mediates between the living and the dead? Who preys on whom, and along which lines of power, gender, class or nation? Does the undead speak differently? Where does it travel?
  3. Transparent, reusable tools. An open pipeline that other researchers can run on their own texts and languages, with annotation interfaces that do not require programming.
  4. Accountable interpretation. Every automatic proposal checked by a researcher, every correction logged, and every figure traceable back to passages in the text.
  5. Teaching and outreach. Visualisations that students and general readers can explore, and that open a way into the texts themselves.
  6. A research community. A network of people (literary scholars, folklorists, translators, computational linguists) who share corpora, methods and publications.
4. How we work

Method and principles

Every analysis passes through a human-in-the-loop pipeline written in Python. Machines do the large, repetitive work; researchers make the interpretive decisions.

StageWhoWhat happens
ExtractionAutomaticDirect speech, and candidate events in the narration, are pulled from the digitised text.
ValidationResearcherEach line is accepted, corrected, flagged, deleted or added in a browser interface.
AnnotationResearcherSpeaker, addressee, silent participants; gender and rank; aliases of the undead figure; agent, patient and sense of each event.
NormalisationAutomaticName variants merged, composite entries split, corrections applied over the untouched annotation file, every change logged.
ModellingAutomaticNetworks, centralities, communities, vocabulary measures (Stanza), event layers, chapter ranges.
PublicationBothAn interactive visualizer with the provenance of the data, and close reading of what it shows.

Principles

  • Changes go through the pipeline. Any new analysis or correction is made in the pipeline itself, never by hand on the output, so that it is documented and visible in the visualizer.
  • Source files stay untouched. Corrections are applied as separate, reviewable layers.
  • Uncertainty is shown. Small samples are flagged, figurative and literal readings are kept apart, and agreement between the automatic proposals and the researchers' decisions is measured.
  • Comparability first. The same steps and measures for every work, so that differences come from the texts and not from the method.

The full methodology, with the measures we compute, is described on the About page.

5. Data, rights and ethics

Data, rights and ethics

  • Texts. We do not publish the full texts of works still in copyright. The site shows derived data (networks, counts, measures) and only the short quotations needed to document a finding.
  • Code. The pipeline is released under the GNU General Public License v3.0.
  • Data on this site. The analyses are for reading, teaching and research with citation. Automated scraping and use for training AI systems are not permitted, and text and data mining rights are reserved under Art. 4(3) of Directive (EU) 2019/790. Researchers who need the data for a study can ask us directly.
  • Visitors. The site sets no cookies and counts visits anonymously. See Privacy & terms.
  • Credit. Everyone who annotates, validates or adapts the pipeline for a work is credited on that work and in the publications built on it.
6. Outputs

Selected outputs

  • The interactive archive at horrordigitalhumanities.org, with character networks and vocabulary profiles for every work, a predation layer for Vampire Nation, and a translation comparison for Der Fremde.
  • Martin, A.-S., & Martin-Căluțiu, A.-N. “Dracula Inverted”: a computational comparison of Dracula and Vampirul, the first Romanian vampire novel, presented at the Dracula Congress “Children of the Night”, Brașov.
  • Martin, A.-S., Light, D., & Martin-Căluțiu, A.-N. “The Party Runs on Blood: Distant Reading Romania's Communist Past in a Postcommunist Vampire Novel” (in preparation).
  • Martin-Căluțiu, A.-N., & Martin, A.-S. The architecture of the dialogue and character-network pipeline (forthcoming).
7. Roadmap

What comes next

Literary geography
Settings, character mobility, place networks and time, for selected works.
More narrative events
Beyond predation: transformation, burial and return, and other acts central to undead fiction.
More languages
Adapting the pipeline to new languages and to the original texts behind translations.
Comparison tools
Views that set works side by side, across traditions and periods.
Several annotators
Double annotation of the same work, with agreement between researchers measured.
A growing archive
New works proposed and annotated by collaborators.
8. Join us

An invitation to researchers

We would like Undead Networks to become a shared space for everyone who works on the undead in literature. If you study vampires, revenants, ghosts, the Gothic, folklore or horror fiction, in any language, we would be glad to hear from you.

Who we are looking for

  • Literary scholars of horror, the Gothic, the fantastic and folklore
  • Translators, translation scholars and comparatists
  • Computational linguists, digital humanists and developers
  • Teachers who want to use the archive in class, and students looking for a thesis corpus

Ways to take part

Propose a work

Suggest a novel or story for the archive and tell us why it matters.

Annotate and validate

Use our browser interfaces to annotate a work you know well. No programming needed.

Adapt the pipeline

Bring the method to a new language, a new kind of narrative event, or a new measure.

Co-author a study

Design a comparative study across works or traditions and publish it with us.

What we offer

  • The tools, documentation and our help in running them
  • Credit on the work in the archive, and co-authorship of the publications you contribute to
  • A place for your analysis next to comparable works from other traditions

How to join

Write to andreinicolae@tuta.io with the subject “Joining Undead Networks”, and tell us briefly:

  1. who you are and where you work;
  2. the work, language or question you would like to bring;
  3. how you would like to take part.
