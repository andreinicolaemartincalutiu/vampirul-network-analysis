About the project

Mapping the social lives of the undead

Undead Networks reads horror fiction at a distance. We turn novels about vampires, strigoi and revenants into data (who speaks, to whom, in whose presence, with what words, and who feeds on whom) and study the resulting networks alongside close reading of the texts.

Overview

A distant reading project on undead literature

Undead Networks is a digital humanities project that studies vampiric and revenant figures across literary traditions, from the strigoi of Romanian folklore and fiction to the Gothic canon, the pulp magazines of the early twentieth century and contemporary satire.

Each novel or story in the archive is converted into a structured dataset of its dialogue. From that dataset we build two complementary analyses: a character network showing who interacts with whom, and a vocabulary profile showing how each character speaks and how their language changes from one interlocutor to another. Newer strands read the narration as well (what characters do to one another, starting with acts of predation) and compare translations of the same work. Literary geography is in development.

The project is based in Sibiu, Romania, and was founded by Anca-Simina Martin and Andrei-Nicolae Martin-Căluțiu.

Purpose

Why study the undead as a network?

A vampire story is almost always a story about relationships. The undead figure feeds on a household, seduces or haunts a chosen victim, and spreads through a community. Often it is noticed less through its own words than through what others say about it. These patterns of intimacy, contagion, isolation and exclusion are social structures, and social structures can be mapped.

Modelling each text as a network of characters lets us ask questions that are hard to answer by reading alone:

  1. Where does the monster sit in the social fabric of the story? Is it a central hub, a bridge between otherwise separate groups, or a marginal figure that is talked about more than it talks?
  2. Who mediates? Which characters connect the world of the living to the world of the undead?
  3. How do communities form around the threat? Do families, lovers, servants or investigators form distinct clusters, and where do they overlap?
  4. Does the undead speak differently? Is its vocabulary richer or poorer, more exclusive, or more closely tied to particular interlocutors than those of the human characters?
  5. Who preys on whom? Is predation literal or figurative, and does it run along lines of power, class or nation?
  6. How does gender shape the network? Who speaks, who is spoken to, and who is silenced?
  7. How do traditions compare? Does a Romanian strigoi novel organise its world differently from a German Gothic tale or an American pulp story?
  8. Where does the undead travel? Is the monster tied to one place, or does it cross borders and carry its threat with it? Who moves, and who stays behind?

The models are not meant to replace interpretation with numbers. They raise new questions, test critical intuitions, and point readers back to specific passages in the texts.

Corpus

The archive

The archive brings together undead fiction from several traditions: Romanian novels built around the strigoi and the vampire, international fiction set in Romania, and German Gothic and American pulp stories. Every work has a character network and a vocabulary profile; some have further analyses, such as narrative events or a comparison between translations.

Because every text passes through the same pipeline, the works can be compared with one another, and the archive is designed to keep growing as new texts are processed.

Methodology

A human-in-the-loop pipeline

Every analysis is produced by an open-source pipeline built in Python. Computation handles the repetitive, large-scale work. Researchers make the interpretive decisions a machine cannot make reliably: who is speaking, who is being addressed, who is silently present in a scene, and whether an act in the narration means what it seems to mean.

  1. 1

    Dialogue extraction Automatic

    The digitised text is parsed and every line of direct speech is extracted. For Romanian texts, the extractor recognises dialogue introduced by an em-dash (—) and speech in Romanian quotation marks („…”). Short attribution-only fragments such as — zise el ("he said") are filtered out, so the dataset holds only what characters actually say.

  2. 2

    Dialogue validation Researcher

    Each extracted line is reviewed in a dedicated browser interface, where it can be accepted, corrected, flagged, deleted, or supplemented with dialogue the algorithm missed. The result is a verified corpus of utterances that stays faithful to the source text.

  3. 3

    Character assignment Researcher

    For every utterance, a researcher records the speaker, the dialogue partner being addressed, and the silent participants present in the scene. The third category matters especially in undead fiction, where the most important figure in a room is often the one who says nothing.

  4. 4

    Dataset normalisation Automatic

    The annotated material is cleaned into a stable, reusable dataset. Name variants are merged, composite entries ("X and Y") are split into their members, and corrections are applied on top of the annotation file, which itself stays untouched. Every change is logged.

  5. 5

    Character metadata Researcher

    Each character is annotated with gender (male, female, or collective for groups such as villagers or servants) and, where the analysis needs it, a social rank. When an undead figure appears under several names or guises, its aliases can be linked to one identity.

  6. 6

    Network construction Automatic

    The dialogues become a weighted graph. Direct interaction edges connect speakers and addressees, weighted by how often they talk. Co-presence edges connect characters who share a scene without necessarily talking to each other. Centrality measures and communities are then calculated on this graph, for the whole work or for a range of chapters.

  7. 7

    Vocabulary analysis Automatic

    All dialogue is tokenised, tagged for part of speech and lemmatised with Stanza, Stanford's natural language processing library. Profiles are built from content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs), with stopwords removed, for each character and for each relationship.

  8. 8

    Narrative events Researcher New

    The narration, not only the dialogue, is searched for acts of a given kind. For predation, a lexicon of verbs (drink, suck, bite, drain, feed, bleed…) and nouns (blood, fang, prey…) proposes candidates, and Stanza's dependency parse suggests who is the agent and who the patient. A researcher then validates every candidate: confirms or corrects agent and patient, and labels the act as literal, figurative or not predation at all.

Network measures

What the network tells us

Standard social network analysis measures describe each character's position in the story's social world.

Degree centrality
How many other characters someone is connected to.
Closeness centrality
How close a character is, on average, to everyone else in the story.
Betweenness centrality
How often a character lies on the path between two others, acting as a bridge or go-between.
Eigenvector centrality
Whether a character is connected to other well-connected, important characters.
Communities
Groups of characters who interact mainly with each other, found by greedy modularity optimisation.
Edge weight
The strength of a relationship, measured by the number of exchanges or shared scenes.
Vocabulary measures

How the characters speak

The vocabulary analysis looks at the language of each character and of each relationship.

Type–token ratio (TTR)
The share of distinct words in a character's speech; a simple measure of lexical variety.
MTLD
Measure of Textual Lexical Diversity. Less sensitive than TTR to how much a character speaks, so better for comparison.
Part-of-speech profile
Whether a character's speech leans towards naming, acting or describing.
Top and exclusive words
Most frequent words, and words a character uses only with a particular partner.
Vocabulary overlap
Jaccard similarity between a relationship's vocabulary and the character's overall vocabulary: does someone speak differently depending on who is listening?
Reliability flag
Measures from small samples are unreliable. Relationships under 30 words of dialogue are marked accordingly.
Narrative events

Who feeds on whom

Narrative events add a directed layer to the network: an arrow from the agent of an act to its patient. For predation, the visualizer shows these arrows in a dedicated view, solid for literal acts and dashed when every act between two characters is figurative.

Predation events
Validated acts of feeding, biting, draining or bleeding, each tied to its sentence in the text.
Literal / figurative
Whether a body is fed on, or a nation, class or person is bled in a political or moral sense.
Predation ratio
For each character or social rank, agent ÷ (agent + patient): 1 means it only preys, 0 that it is only preyed on.
Agreement
How often the researcher's decision confirms the automatic proposal: a measure of how far the extraction can be trusted on its own.
Using the site

Exploring the visualisations

Each network is an interactive, force-directed graph built with D3.js. In each view you can:

  • filter characters by number of dialogues and relationships by strength;
  • switch between direct-dialogue and co-presence links;
  • collapse the network into its detected communities;
  • merge the different names of the undead figure into a single node;
  • narrow the network to a range of chapters, hide collective characters, or open the predation view, where the work supports it;
  • click any character to open a panel with their network statistics, connections, vocabulary profile, relationship-specific word use, community cohesion and, where available, their role in predation;
  • reset every filter in one click, and export any view as SVG or PNG for teaching, presentations or publication.

We encourage readers to move between the graph and the text, and to test what they see against their own reading.

Open science

Open source and reusable

The full pipeline, from extraction through the annotation interfaces to network generation and visualisation, is released under the GNU General Public License v3.0.

It was first built for Romanian novels but can be adapted to other languages and corpora by replacing the input text, the character list, the attribution verbs, the stopword list and the Stanza language model. We welcome researchers, teachers and students who want to apply it to their own texts, extend the archive, or propose comparative studies.

The code is open; the texts and the derived data shown on this site are not offered for automated harvesting. See our terms of use.

In development

Literary geography and mobility

Undead fiction is also a fiction of places: the remote castle, the village at the edge of the forest, the manor, the city the monster reaches from abroad. Alongside the character networks, we are developing geographical analyses for selected works in the archive.

  • Settings: identifying the real and imagined places each work mentions or moves through, and mapping them.
  • Character mobility: tracing how characters move between places, which characters travel and which remain fixed, and whether the undead figure is bound to a place or crosses borders.
  • Place networks: linking locations through the characters who move between them, to show how a story's world is connected.
  • Time: following how settings and movements shift over the course of the narrative and across periods and traditions.

These analyses will appear in the archive alongside the existing studies as they become available.
